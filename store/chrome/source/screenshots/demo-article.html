How browsers decide what to paint first

Before a single pixel appears, the browser has already made dozens of quiet decisions about what matters most to you.

By Mara Lindqvist · 12 min read

When you open a page, the browser doesn't wait for every file to arrive. It starts parsing HTML as soon as the first bytes come in, building the document tree piece by piece while the rest of the response is still on its way.

Stylesheets are a different story. Because a late rule could change how anything on the page looks, the browser holds back the first paint until the CSS it already knows about has loaded. This is why a slow stylesheet can leave a page blank even when the HTML arrived quickly.

The viewport comes first

Modern engines are careful about where they spend their effort. Content inside the viewport is laid out and painted before anything further down, and images below the fold can wait until the reader scrolls toward them.

The fastest work is the work you never have to do, and the second fastest is the work you do only when someone is about to look.

You can help the browser with a few hints. Mark offscreen images with loading="lazy", keep critical styles small, and avoid scripts that block parsing near the top of the document.

Measuring what readers see

Largest Contentful Paint measures when the biggest element in the viewport finishes rendering. It is a better proxy for "the page looks ready" than the old load event, which waits for everything, including content nobody has scrolled to yet.

Once the first frame is on screen, the browser keeps scheduling work around the reader: it fetches what is about to scroll into view, pauses timers in background tabs, and drops frames that would never be seen.
